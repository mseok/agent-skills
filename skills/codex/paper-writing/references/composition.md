# Compose the paper

Read the author's original message and authorized writing references, the brief, the complete research facts, and the complete named published openings. Include corrections and contrary findings. Preserve the scientific meaning and binding author decisions while choosing the prose and organization. The message states what the paper should communicate; it need not be the Introduction's first sentence. When no message is supplied, use the provisional one in the brief.

Develop the relationships that make the message scientifically intelligible. Explain how a property, observation or constraint creates the question and how the approach responds. Use literature to establish premises and credit accomplishments. Choose concrete scientific objects, representations and operations when they carry this explanation. When a contribution spans settings, explain the relevant variation and what is shared. Give controls their warranted role. Learn from the named openings' reasoning and disciplinary usage without adopting their motivating problem.

For example, in a hypothetical imaging study, coarse images indicate where rare cells are likely to occur. Saying that imaging is costly establishes the stakes. Explaining how coarse localization directs high-resolution acquisition toward those regions connects the information to an experimental decision. That rationale follows from the procedure; an improvement in detection still needs outcome evidence. Transfer this explanatory move, not its wording or subject.

For an Introduction, normally establish a consequential scientific problem, then develop the difficulty or opportunity that makes this particular question follow. The opening movement should motivate the question at the scientific scope of the contribution. Introduce a system or comparison when its significance is intelligible; it can open the paper when it supplies that significance itself. Choose paragraph boundaries, explanatory depth and result detail from the argument, without a fixed template or quota. A useful empirical or methodological advance can carry a paper without a new general principle.

Write in the field's language. Organize observations into findings and explain their supported scientific meaning. Name the actual quantities, units, comparators and conditions so the finding expresses its scope; keep consequential exceptions beside the claims they change. If a separate qualification answers a broader inference invited earlier, check whether that earlier wording expresses the intended claim. Repair overbroad wording and retain any qualification still needed. Let established context carry forward. Preserve uncertainty and the distinction between observation and interpretation. Use only supplied facts and named sources; do not invent missing support. Record derived values separately with operands, units and basis. Use the specified citation placeholder or author issue for a consequential gap.

Write the complete requested passage, then read it continuously for meaning and progression. Make the question, contribution and answer intelligible in the same terms; repair missing connections, abrupt changes of subject and repetition. Follow the requested format and length. For scoped revisions, preserve the authorized passages and decisions and mark proposed changes inline. Return the requested prose and actionable author issues; keep planning and verification records outside the manuscript. The coordinator owns the subsequent factual and full reference checks.
