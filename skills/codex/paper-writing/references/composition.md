# Compose the paper

Use this card with the supplied editorial brief, complete research facts and named published openings. The coordinator has prepared the task and will verify the draft separately. Read all the supplied facts, including corrections and contrary findings, and the complete named openings before composing. Keep the scientific meaning and all binding author decisions; choose the prose and organization that best develop them.

Write a paper the intended readers have a scientific reason to read. Connect the author's purpose to a live issue in the field, explain what the nearest work already makes possible, and develop the contribution as an answer to that issue. For a method, explain its organizing idea through the scientific difficulty or decision it addresses. Show why the useful addition matters. Let comparative controls establish the contribution and its conditions at the weight their findings warrant. A method inventory or a sequence of comparisons does not by itself supply this reasoning.

Use the literature to build the argument: a study can establish a premise, demonstrate a capability, distinguish an explanation or leave a consequential question. Credit the actual accomplishment and identify the present addition without invented novelty or failure. Learn from the named examples' reasoning, explanatory depth and disciplinary usage; create this paper's own progression and wording.

Write in the field's language. Name the scientific objects, actions and consequences. Organize comparisons into the finding they support; connect a metric to the scientific distinction it measures when the record permits. State a result with the conditions that make it true, and keep a consequential exception beside the claim it changes. Once a setting is established in a connected passage, let it carry forward. Explain what occurred and what it means without narrating how the draft was checked.

Select detail by its role in the argument. Give enough explanation for these readers to understand the contribution and enough evidence to grasp the answer. For an Introduction, normally begin with the consequential broader scientific problem that motivates the study, then narrow to the specific question, approach and evidence. Let each narrowing step give the reader a reason for the next. Introduce particular systems and comparisons when they explain the answer; open with one only when that specific problem itself supplies the paper's significance and fits the author's direction. Choose paragraph boundaries and detail from the argument, without a fixed template or result quota. Keep necessary uncertainty and material contrary evidence. A useful empirical or methodological advance can carry a paper without a new general principle.

Every factual claim, number and citation must come from the supplied record or named source material. Preserve units, comparators, conditions, uncertainty and the distinction between observation and interpretation. Explain consequences that follow from the stated premises; do not turn a plausible mechanism into an experimental finding. If you derive a value, record the calculation separately with operands, units and basis. Use the specified citation placeholder for missing support. Record a consequential unresolved fact separately for the author. Do not invent a result, motive, mechanism, priority claim or literature shortfall to complete the argument.

Write the complete requested passage, then read it continuously as a manuscript. Make its scientific question, contribution and answer intelligible in the same terms; repair missing premises, abrupt changes of subject and repetition. Follow the brief's format and length requirements. For a scoped revision, keep the authorized passages and decisions, and mark proposed changes inline as instructed. Return the requested prose and actionable author issues; leave planning and review records to the coordinator.
