# Introduction

Write the argument that makes this study worth reading. The reader should understand the scientific problem, why the available knowledge leaves a consequential question, and what this paper contributes. Use the actual journal, article type and matched examples selected through `venue-adaptation.md`; consult the relevant entry in `journal-profiles.md`.

Use this guide during coordinator preparation and integration. Give the fresh composer the scientific decisions through the brief, alongside `composition.md`, complete facts and the complete named source openings; keep the detailed preparation and audit guides in their own contexts (`delegation.md`).

## Find the argument before arranging the paragraphs

Develop the author's supplied message and framing from the complete record, using a provisional message from `planning.md` only when none is supplied. The brief adds task decisions and any missing premises; the composer develops the exposition and paragraph sequence. Read `argument-example.md` for paired synthetic examples of how different scientific purposes organize the same evidence. Examine the complete opening prose of matched primary articles before a new draft, as specified in `venue-adaptation.md`; summarized style rules alone do not supply this reading.

Explain the contribution within the supplied purpose at the strength the results support. A method can matter because it enables a scientific decision or demonstrates a useful empirical advance; a diagnostic study can matter because it changes how a familiar result is interpreted. A resource can make a previously difficult comparison possible. Do not force these contributions into the same failed-methods story or require a new general principle. In a method or capability paper, use comparative controls to explain the contribution and its conditions; change that hierarchy only when the evidence requires it or the author directs it.

Construct the introduction as connected reasoning. Each paragraph should leave the reader ready for the next. Decide how much background, prior work, explanation and evidence the argument needs; only then choose paragraph boundaries. State the contribution when its meaning is intelligible. Related work may precede or follow that statement when the sequence helps explain it.

## Opening and importance

Normally begin with the consequential broader scientific problem that motivates the study and narrow naturally toward its specific question. Choose a starting scale at which the intended readers can understand what is at stake, then explain the difficulty that makes a more specific question necessary. Breadth should supply a useful premise; a general declaration that a field is important does not do this work.

Establish why the research matters before introducing its particular test systems, datasets or experimental comparisons. These can make the approach and evidence concrete once the motivating problem is clear. Begin with a specific system only when that problem itself carries the paper's significance and fits the author's direction; an available or vivid example is not sufficient.

Let the narrowing follow the scientific relationships: what matters, what makes progress difficult, what existing work makes possible, and what question or opportunity remains. Paragraph boundaries and explanatory depth follow that reasoning. Do not turn the progression into fixed paragraph slots or invent a failure of prior work to complete it.

## Literature as reasoning

Describe the relevant accomplishment of prior work in the terms needed for this paper's question. Group studies by a meaningful relationship, such as the information they use, the scientific decision they enable, the assumption they share or the explanation they support. Discuss individual methods when their distinction matters; avoid a catalogue in which each sentence merely names another system.

Make the remaining question follow from what is already possible. Existing demonstrations may motivate a question about when an approach works, how to organize it, or what explains its performance. Credit those demonstrations directly. Do not invent a missing control, an absent capability or a universal failure to sharpen the gap.

## Contribution and evidence

Explain the contribution in the same scientific terms used to motivate it. For a method, give its organizing idea and the operations necessary to understand that idea. Explain the method through the decision it enables or the scientific difficulty it addresses. An operational rationale describes what the procedure does; a claim that a component caused an empirical benefit requires suitable evidence. A list of all components, input fields, fallback behaviors or software interfaces is usually a Methods description. Keep a detail here when it explains the scientific advance.

Select a preview that helps the reader grasp the answer. This may be a qualitative finding, a capability, a contrast or a decisive quantitative result. Give a comparator, budget, unit or condition when needed to interpret a claim; leave the rest of the result table for Results. Use no quota for numerical results. End on the contribution's scientific meaning when the argument supports it, rather than repeating the study design or appending a generic future promise.

Keep a contrary finding when it changes the message. Express it as part of the scientific pattern: identify where an effect occurs, what information matters or which comparison changes. Do not announce that the evidence is mixed or follow an accurate finding with a list of things it does not prove. Context established in a connected passage carries forward without repeated scope labels.

## Read as a manuscript

Read the whole introduction without consulting the checklist. Is the study compelling and intelligible, and does the argument keep moving? Repair a missing reason, an abrupt transition or a misplaced paragraph before polishing individual words. Then check terminology, factual meaning and citation support.

Use established disciplinary nouns and verbs, explaining unfamiliar concepts when the readership needs them. Let sentence length follow the reasoning. An acronym, technical expression or familiar scholarly construction earns its place through clarity, not through a universal style rule. Preserve meaningful uncertainty; remove redundant framing and bookkeeping. The published examples illustrate editorial choices, not phrases to transplant or layouts to reproduce.
