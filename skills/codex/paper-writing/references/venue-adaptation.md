# Adapt the paper to its journal and readers

Use this for a new paper, a changed target or a substantial reframing. The author supplies the research direction and target; obtain the relevant public material and make ordinary editorial choices yourself. Keep the working context compact, reuse it within the project, and never require the author to build a journal profile or terminology file.

## Establish the context

Identify the exact journal or conference, article type, field, intended readers and actual contribution. Infer an unspecified article type from the work and verify that the target uses it. Respect the author's stated direction; ask only about an ambiguity that would materially change the paper.

Read the target's current official scope, editorial criteria and instructions for the relevant article type. Separate formal requirements from editorial considerations, observed examples and your own choices. Record relevant URLs and access dates in working notes. Do not treat journal family, prestige, citation count or acceptance rate as a writing specification.

The coordinator may reuse the target-resolution, primary-source and comparison-context guidance in `../../journal-editor-review/references/journal-discovery.md` when available. Do not invoke that skill's review panel, submission screening, verdict or bibliometrics as part of writing. An isolated writer uses the supplied brief; the coordinator obtains sources before delegation.

## Read matched examples

Select a small useful set of published papers, often three to five initially, matched by article type, scientific area, contribution and evidential breadth. Include different plausible arguments when useful. Prefer recent examples for current terminology and format; an older paper may clarify a lasting editorial choice. Avoid selecting only famous or unusually expansive papers.

Read complete Introductions and abstracts, then the Results, Methods or Discussion passages needed to understand how their central claims are supported. An abstract or search snippet is not a substitute for the Introduction. If access is incomplete, use an accessible author manuscript or another appropriate example and record what was actually read. Continue useful writing without pretending missing material was inspected.

Extract decisions, not phrases to imitate:

- the scientific question and why these readers care;
- what the reader is assumed to know and what is explained;
- each introductory paragraph's role and the reason for its position;
- how the contribution relates to existing knowledge or practice;
- where technical explanation becomes necessary;
- how results establish the significance claimed in the title and abstract;
- disciplinary nouns, verbs, collocations and distinctions that carry scientific meaning.

A few examples reveal possible arguments, not a journal-wide template. Keep official requirements, observed choices and the strategy chosen for this paper distinct in working notes.

## Choose the central question and argument

Find the most consequential question for these readers that the supplied work actually answers. Consider whether the contribution changes understanding, enables a useful capability, resolves a disagreement, improves a measurement or method, establishes a boundary, or supplies a resource. These are possibilities, not a mandatory taxonomy. A specialist question can be ambitious; a broad journal does not require a grand opening.

When the direction is not already settled, compare plausible framings privately and select the best supported one. Do not automatically describe a substantive finding as an implementation change, or enlarge a limited comparison into a demonstrated practical benefit. Tie the selected message to actual results in working notes. Keep evidence-status labels and inventories of untested extensions out of the prose.

Outline the argument by what each paragraph enables the reader to understand next. A methods paper may explain a limitation and the insight that addresses it; another paper may begin with competing explanations, conflicting measurements, an unexplained phenomenon or a missing resource. Use only the moves this paper needs. Paragraph counts, contribution lists and closing formulas follow the argument and target format.

If the author's intended claim exceeds the available results, write the strongest accurate version and report the concrete issue briefly to the author. Do not pad the manuscript with disclaimers, invent findings, silently change the target or stop all writing because one passage needs a fact.

## Write in the field's language

Name the scientific entities and actions: samples, structures, conformations, measurements, interventions, predictions and the quantities actually evaluated. Use established terms consistently; related quantities such as affinity and functional activity are not stylistic synonyms. Prefer the author's terminology when accurate.

Replace generic software or review language with the scientific operation it denotes. For example, describe generating variants and measuring binding when those are the supplied facts, rather than "orchestrating candidates and downstream validation". Keep a computational term when it is the accepted, precise term for the contribution. Do not use a universal banned-word list or mimic an exemplar author's distinctive sentences.

Match explanation to the target readers. Explain a technical distinction early when it is essential to understand the advance; place implementation details where they support assessment or reproduction. Do not turn a specialist paper into a tutorial or remove its scientific reasoning in pursuit of broad accessibility.

## Integrate and reuse

Use the selected question and reader assumptions across the title, abstract, Introduction, Results and Discussion. Check both overstatement and understatement of the contribution. Preserve meaning through the surrounding scientific context rather than repeating qualifiers. Apply the target's actual section, abstract, heading and supplementary-material requirements.

Keep useful source pointers and editorial choices in the existing plan or brief. Reuse them for passage revisions; refresh them when the target, contribution, article type or relevant source changes. A source used as a writing example is not automatically a scientific citation in the manuscript.

## Contrasting published examples

These illustrate choices, not fixed profiles. Abstracts and full Introductions were inspected on 2026-11-26; the examples do not constitute a full assessment of the experiments.

- **ProteinGenerator, Nature Biotechnology** ([published article](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41587-024-02395-w), [author-hosted full text](https://www.ipd.uw.edu/publication-pdfs/312/deaab1fe65adbe2dc10584032b687631/s41587-024-02395-w-1.pdf)): a two-paragraph Introduction connects a sequence–structure design constraint to the proposed capability and introduces technical concepts immediately. Learn when technical explanation establishes significance, not a requirement for short Introductions.
- **Efficient evolution of human antibodies from general protein language models, Nature Biotechnology** ([full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10869273/)): the argument asks whether general evolutionary information can guide improvement under specific selection pressures. Learn to identify a scientific question within a computational application, not to append claims of therapeutic impact.
- **Analyzing Learned Molecular Representations for Property Prediction, JCIM** ([author-hosted full text](https://people.csail.mit.edu/tommi/papers/acs.jcim.9b00237.pdf)): conflicting comparisons motivate a question about evaluation in new chemical space. Learn how a field-specific uncertainty establishes importance while results remain conditional. This 2019 example is not evidence of the journal's current typical style.
